# **_The Team_**

Success in technical transformation requires building trust not just with decision makers, but with everyone involved in implementation. We aspire to approach these relationships through what Charles H. Green calls the “trust equation” in his work on trusted advisory relationships:

_Trustworthiness \= (Credibility \+ Reliability \+ Intimacy) / Self-Interest_

While credibility should come from our technical expertise, reliability from our consistent delivery, and intimacy from understanding each person's context and concerns. We've also found that minimizing self-interest is particularly crucial when working with customer teams. These team members need to know that we're genuinely invested in their long-term success rather than just completing a project.

Beyond executive sponsors and key decision-makers, the success of any technical transformation depends heavily on our ability to understand and work effectively with the entire customer team. These teams often include architects, engineers, managers, and subject matter experts who bring their own perspectives, concerns, and working styles to the engagement. We should pay particular attention to team dynamics and composition, as even the strongest executive support cannot overcome deep-seated team resistance or dysfunction.

Drawing inspiration from Terrence Ryan's "Driving Technical Change," there are several distinct personality types that regularly emerge in technical transformations. Our success often depends on recognizing these personalities early and adapting our approach accordingly.

**"The Curious"** often serve as intellectual bridges within the team. These individuals may be current system owners or senior engineers who want to thoroughly understand new approaches before embracing them. Their questions like "why would we structure it this way?" or "how does this compare to our current approach?" can create valuable teaching moments for the entire team. We should cultivate their curiosity by breaking down complex concepts into clear fundamentals, demonstrating ideas through working prototypes, and welcoming deep technical discussions.

**"The Innovators"** act as internal champions for positive change. Whether they're tech leads or individual contributors, they see the potential of new technologies to solve longstanding problems. They engage actively in architectural discussions, contribute fresh perspectives, and help others see beyond immediate constraints. We should support them by balancing their enthusiasm with pragmatic implementation approaches, helping them evaluate emerging technologies responsibly, and sharing relevant experiences from similar transformations.

**"The Mentors"** focus on their teams' long-term growth. Often found in technical leadership roles, they care deeply about building lasting capabilities rather than just delivering features. They should value our emphasis on knowledge transfer, pair programming, and comprehensive documentation. Their involvement would help ensure that new approaches and technologies take root within the organization rather than remaining dependent on external expertise.

**"The Pragmatists"** keep transformation efforts grounded in reality and can often begin as detractors if they perceive proposed solutions as overly complex or academic. Found at all levels of the organization, they might object with concerns like "this is overkill for our needs" or "we need something simpler that just works." We should earn their trust through measurable outcomes, regular progress updates, and clear ties between technical decisions and business impact. Their practical focus helps ensure that changes deliver tangible benefits rather than just technical elegance.

**"The Skeptics"** typically begin as detractors but can bring valuable caution to the process. Often senior team members who have experienced failed transformations, their initial objections might include "we've tried this before and it didn't work" or "this is just another fad." To win them over, we should acknowledge their past experiences, address concerns through small, concrete wins, and provide clear evidence for technical decisions. Their skepticism, when converted to constructive criticism, can help strengthen our approaches.

**"The Defenders"** often start as our strongest detractors, and understandably so. Whether they originally built these systems or have maintained them for years, they may view outside consultants as a threat to their domain expertise and authority. Common objections might include "our system works fine as it is," "outsiders don't understand our unique requirements," or "we can handle this internally." To win them over, we should actively acknowledge their deep system knowledge, involve them early in architectural decisions, and position ourselves as partners rather than replacements. Their expertise is crucial for ensuring smooth transitions, and by showing genuine respect for their experience while carefully addressing their concerns, we can often transform initial skepticism into valuable collaboration.

When evaluating potential engagements, we should pay particular attention to team dynamics and composition. Teams that balance healthy skepticism with openness to new ideas, respect for existing knowledge, and commitment to growth provide fertile ground for lasting positive change. Our most successful engagements should combine supportive leadership with engaged, collaborative teams representing many of these complementary personalities.

**Our Proven Process**

A proven process helps customers understand exactly how you deliver value, giving them confidence and peace of mind while differentiating you from competitors. The process should be visual, memorable, and capture the major steps that represent your customer touchpoints. Our proven process, which we call "The Spantree Way," reflects our unique approach to technical consulting that emphasizes both delivery excellence and collaborative learning.

The Spantree Way begins with understanding your world. The journey starts with a **Ride Along**, where we spend time with your team observing operations, interviewing stakeholders, and experiencing your business challenges firsthand. This no-cost, low-commitment approach helps us identify opportunities where technology can drive significant business value. In exchange for our time and expertise, we ask for meaningful access to your teams and processes.

Once we've identified a specific opportunity, we begin our formal discovery phase, which we refer to as **Iteration Zero.** We use tools like Wardley Maps to understand your strategic landscape and Event Storming to visualize complex processes. Through technical spikes and collaborative workshops, we validate approaches and create a detailed plan for our initial six-week delivery cycle. Our architects and engineers work to deeply understand both the technical constraints and business context before any code is written.

Our delivery model balances structure with flexibility. During our **Progressive Implementation Phase**, we execute in six-week delivery cycles broken into two-week sprints. We emphasize regular pair programming, continuous feedback, and scope calibration to maximize value delivery. Throughout implementation, we conduct regular validation sessions to review both technical and business goals, using scope hammering \- the art of finding the essential elements of a feature \- to ensure we're delivering the highest value within our fixed-budget, scope-controlled model.

Success means leaving your team stronger than we found it. We conclude with **Transition & Growth**, focusing on ensuring your team's long-term success through comprehensive knowledge transfer, documentation, and operational handoff. What makes The Spantree Way unique is how it combines technical rigor with collaborative learning at every step, reflecting our commitment to not just building great software, but building great teams.

While all good things must eventually come to an end, we strive to find new challenges and opportunities to **work with our customers on their next hard problem**. These efforts may often be driven by the yet-unexplored future directions identified during the ride along, but it is also often influenced by observations we’ve made throughout the delivery process.
